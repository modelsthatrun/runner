A workshop on the edge of a small town kept a bench for testing instruments. Nothing on the bench was new. The voltmeters had scratched faces, the calipers had been dropped more than once, and the oscilloscope hummed for a minute before its trace settled. People brought in tools they had bought secondhand and asked one question: does it still measure what it says it measures? The people who ran the bench had learned that the honest answer always came with conditions attached, so they wrote the conditions down first and the answer second.

The first condition was the room. A thermometer hung by the door, and every reading in the logbook began with the temperature, the humidity and the time of day. Metal expands when it warms, batteries sag when they cool, and a gauge that is perfect at noon can drift by evening. If two readings were taken on different days, the logbook made it possible to say whether the instrument had changed or only the room had.

The second condition was the reference. Every tool was compared against something whose accuracy was known and traceable: a set of gauge blocks in a wooden case, a voltage standard kept in a padded box, a clock that set itself from a radio signal every night. The references were checked against outside laboratories once a year, and the certificates were pinned to the wall above the bench. A measurement without a reference, the owner liked to say, is only an opinion with a number attached.

The third condition was repetition. A single reading proved very little, because hands shake, contacts corrode and electrical noise comes and goes. The bench measured everything at least three times, wrote down every result, and reported the middle one along with the spread. When the spread was wide, the report said so plainly, and the customer was told that the instrument could not be trusted to better than that width, however many digits its display showed.

The fourth condition was patience. Many instruments read differently in their first minutes after being switched on. Tubes and crystals need to warm, springs need to settle, and the first few readings are often outliers. The bench let every instrument run for a while before the first reading counted, and it threw away those early readings without regret, because they described the warming and not the instrument.

The fifth condition was separation. A reading taken by the bench was never mixed with a figure printed on the box. The box might promise an accuracy of one part in a thousand, and the bench might find one part in five hundred. Both numbers went on the report, each with its label, so the customer could see the promise and the measurement side by side and decide for themselves which one mattered.

Over the years the logbooks filled a shelf. Anyone could open them and follow a single instrument through a decade of visits: a micrometer that slowly lost its zero, a meter that was perfect until a storm, a scale that never changed at all. The logbooks were worth more than any single report, because they showed that the method had stayed the same while the instruments came and went. A number from the first logbook could be compared with a number from the last only because the conditions written above them were the same.

Customers sometimes asked for shortcuts. Could the bench skip the warm-up, just this once? Could it report the best reading instead of the middle one? Could it leave out the spread, since the spread made the instrument look worse? The answer was always no, and the reason was always the same. A shortcut taken for one customer would make every other report on the shelf mean a little less, and the shelf was the only thing the bench had to offer.

When the bench began testing more complicated machines, the conditions stayed the same. A machine that sorted parts by size was timed over many runs, with the first runs set aside. Its memory use was watched from outside, so that the act of watching did not slow it down. Its results were compared against a sorted reference set prepared by hand, and the share of parts it placed correctly was written next to the share the manufacturer had claimed. The machines were faster and stranger than the old voltmeters, but the logbook entries looked almost exactly like the old ones, and that was the point.
